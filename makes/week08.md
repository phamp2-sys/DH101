## Process Notes
**How did you make this?**  
I created a network map of the AI supply chain, tracing connections from raw materials and chip manufacturers to cloud infrastructure, AI companies, workers, and users. I collected information from company websites and reports, organized the relationships in Google Sheets, and imported the data into Kumu to visualize the network.

**What tools did you use?**  
I used Google Sheets to organize the nodes, connections, relationship types, and sources. I used Kumu to build and arrange the network visualization. I also consulted company websites and International Energy Agency (IEA) reports to research the relationships.

**What decisions did you make?**  
I chose to focus on the economic side of AI rather than only its technology. I included corporations and infrastructure providers alongside data workers, natural resources, and local communities to show that AI depends on more than the companies developing it. I organized the network around relationships such as supplying materials, providing infrastructure, and supporting AI development. I also aimed to distinguish major companies from less visible contributors, highlighting how power and value are distributed unevenly throughout the AI supply chain.

## Reflection
Network Analysis
My network maps AI as an economic system rather than an isolated technological product. Its connections extend from critical mineral processing and semiconductor manufacturing to cloud infrastructure, AI applications, human labor, and the communities that provide essential resources.
Centrality. NVIDIA is a likely central node in this selected network because it connects semiconductor manufacturing to the computing infrastructure used for AI. Microsoft and its Azure platform are also important because the cloud infrastructure relationship connects corporate resources to AI development. However, centrality depends on what the graph includes. Degree centrality measures direct connections, while betweenness centrality identifies nodes that bridge otherwise separate groups. I would compare these measures in Kumu rather than assume that the most recognizable company is automatically the most powerful.
Clusters. Three broad clusters emerge: the hardware and materials supply chain; cloud platforms and AI developers; and the human and environmental systems supporting AI. These groups are not independent. For example, the cloud and model-development cluster depends on physical infrastructure, electricity, and specialized hardware. Data annotators also contribute to AI development, even though their work is less visible in consumer-facing products.
What surprised me. The network suggests that AI's apparent convenience depends on an extensive system of interdependence. A user can open ChatGPT in seconds without seeing the chip manufacturers, cloud providers, electricity systems, or human work that make the interaction possible.
What is missing. This graph cannot fully represent informal labor, subcontractors, workers' bargaining power, local residents' experiences, or the distribution of profits. It also simplifies global supply chains into directed links, although real relationships can involve several intermediaries and competing suppliers. Some connections represent industry-wide dependencies rather than exclusive contracts.
The network therefore reveals both concentration and omission. Its most connected companies are relatively easy to identify, while the people and places supporting their operations are harder to represent. These absences are not proof that those groups lack influence. They may instead indicate that the available data privileges corporate disclosures over workers' and communities' perspectives.

Artist Statement
I began this project with a familiar experience: opening an AI tool to complete an assignment, ask an economics question, or generate an image. The interaction feels almost weightless. I wanted to investigate what disappears when technology is presented as something that exists only on a screen.
My network follows the connections between mineral processing, semiconductor production, cloud infrastructure, AI development, human data work, and resource consumption. I use color to distinguish these groups and different edge styles to separate material supply, infrastructure, ownership, and financial relationships. The intention is not simply to display complexity, but to make dependencies visible.
Corporate actors occupy recognizable positions because their products, partnerships, and financial activities are documented. By contrast, data annotators, local communities, and the people affected by electricity and water demand are more difficult to represent. They are not necessarily unimportant or powerless. Their relative invisibility reflects which relationships are recorded, publicized, and made measurable.
This is where visualization becomes political. A graph centered on corporate partnerships might portray AI as a story of innovation and investment. A graph centered on workers, resource extraction, and local communities might instead portray it as a story about labor, unequal exposure to costs, and accountability. Both could use accurate information while producing different interpretations.
I approached the network through an economic lens because I wanted to ask not only who builds AI, but who captures its value and who bears its costs. The graph cannot answer these questions completely, especially when financial flows and informal labor are poorly documented. However, its gaps are meaningful. They remind me that making a network is not a neutral act: deciding what to connect, measure, and display also determines whose experiences become visible.
